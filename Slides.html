Distributed Database Concepts · Chapter 23Part 1 · Member 113
Now some system properties. First, two words that sound the same but are not: reliability and availability. Think about an ATM. [click] Reliability asks: is the ATM working right now, at this moment? It is the probability the system is up at a certain point in time. [click] Availability asks: was the ATM working the whole weekend, continuously? It is the probability the system stays available over a time interval. On the timeline: reliability looks at a single point; availability looks at a whole stretch. A real-world number: when a bank or a cloud provider promises "99.9 percent availability", that still allows about 8.8 hours of downtime per year; "99.99 percent" allows under an hour. That's why they replicate data across sites. Distributed databases improve both, because if one site goes down the others keep working. In everyday writing people often use "availability" for both ideas, but the book separates them like this.

Distributed Database Concepts · Chapter 23Part 1 · Member 114
Why does a system go down? The book uses a chain of three words. A fault is the cause — a defect. An error is a wrong internal state caused by that fault. A failure is when the behaviour the user sees is different from what was specified. Cooking example: the recipe has a typo, "5 spoons of salt" instead of "1" — that is the fault. [click] The soup in the pot now has too much salt — that is the error, the wrong state. [click] The guests taste it and it's bad — that is the failure. [click] There are two ways to deal with this. Fault tolerance accepts that faults will happen and detects and removes them before they become failures — for example, by keeping copies. Fault-free design tries to prevent faults through very careful design and testing, which is much stricter and more expensive. In a DDB, recovery must handle transaction failures, hardware failures like losing memory or a disk, and network failures: messages that are lost, corrupted, or arrive out of order.

Distributed Database Concepts · Chapter 23Part 1 · Member 118
So why go to all this trouble? Four advantages. [click] One, easier application development. Because distribution is transparent, building and maintaining apps across many locations is easier. Distributed Database Concepts · Chapter 23Part 2 · Member 224
A set of horizontal fragments should pass two checks. Complete: every row of the table must land in at least one fragment. Mathematically, every tuple satisfies C1 or C2 or ... Cn. No row is lost. Three-phase commit fixes this. It splits the second phase into two sub-phases: prepare-to-commit and commit. [click] After everyone votes yes, the coordinator first sends PREPARE-TO-COMMIT, sometimes called precommit. This message says only one thing: "everyone voted yes." Distributed Database Concepts · Chapter 23Part 5 · Member 581
Last topic: the catalog. A catalog is itself a database — the metadata saying which tables, fragments and copies exist, and where. Think of a company phone directory. There are three schemes. [click] Centralized: the whole catalog sits at one site — one directory at head office. Easy to build, but it hurts reliability, availability and autonomy. A remote read locks the entry at the centre, copies it, then sends an acknowledgment to unlock it, and every update goes through the centre — a bottleneck for write-heavy applications. [click] Fully replicated: a complete copy at every site — a printed directory in every office. Reads are local and fast, but every update is broadcast to all sites as a transaction under centralized two-phase commit, so many writes mean heavy network traffic. [click] Partially replicated: each site keeps the full catalog for its own data and may cache entries from other sites, with no guarantee they're fresh. Changes go straight to the object's birth site — where it was created — and stale caches are refreshed on next use. Synonyms for remote objects keep transparency. This scheme preserves site autonomy.
